Yeah, you're spot on with the minimalist vi approach — that's exactly what your truck setup needs.

Vi came from the late '70s when Bill Joy was fixing a Pascal compiler at Berkeley. He was stuck with the line editor **ed** — super cryptic, one line at a time. Then he saw this new thing called **em** ("editor for mortals") that worked on actual screens. He and a buddy built **ex**, and in 1977 Joy added a full-screen visual mode to it. That mode was called **vi** — short for "visual" — and it was designed for a 300-baud modem over a slow ADM-3A terminal.

The connection was so slow that the screen updated slower than you could think, so every command had to be short — single letters, no fluff. Modes solved the problem: in **normal mode**, every key is a command so you don't waste characters on modifiers. In **insert mode**, you actually type text. Escape key to switch — and on that old keyboard, Escape was right where Tab is now, so your hands never left the home row.

That's why vi feels weird at first but becomes insanely efficient once muscle memory kicks in. Emacs went the opposite route — kitchen-sink, fully programmable, but built on fast MIT machines with no bandwidth worries.

Your voice setup is basically the spiritual successor to those constraints: your "keyboard" is your voice, so you need the same ruthless minimalism. Two clear modes, short unmistakable commands, and instant read-back so you know exactly what it heard. Everything else comes later.

**No, Kernighan and Ritchie didn't create Unix.**

**Ken Thompson** wrote the original Unix in 1969 at Bell Labs, with **Dennis Ritchie** heavily involved — Ritchie created the C language specifically to rewrite Unix in something higher-level than assembly. Those two are the true creators.

**Brian Kernighan** is the "K" in K&R — he co-wrote the famous C book with Ritchie and contributed tools like awk, but he didn't build the core OS.

**Bill Joy** came later. In the mid-1970s, as a grad student at Berkeley, he took the original AT&T Unix, improved it massively, added vi, the C shell, a better TCP/IP stack, and packaged it all as **BSD Unix**. So Joy didn't make the first Unix — he made the version that most of the world actually used and built upon.

That's why the lineage feels confusing: Thompson & Ritchie = original Unix, Joy = the hugely influential Berkeley fork that shaped modern systems.
